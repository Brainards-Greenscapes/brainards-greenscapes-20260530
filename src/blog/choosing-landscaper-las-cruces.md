Hiring a landscaper seems straightforward until you start getting quotes. You'll probably contact three or four companies, get a range of numbers, and try to figure out why one is half the price of the others. The differences aren't always obvious from a quote — but they show up fast once the work starts.

This is an insider's perspective on what to look for, what to ask, and what should make you walk away. David has been doing landscape work in Las Cruces for years, and he's seen the aftermath of cut-rate jobs often enough to know what goes wrong and why. This guide isn't a sales pitch — it's the same advice he'd give a friend.

## Check Their License

New Mexico requires contractor licensing for landscape installation work through the Construction Industries Division (CID). Before hiring anyone, verify that they hold a current, active license for the type of work you need done.

**How to verify:** Go to the [New Mexico Regulation and Licensing Department's website](https://rld.nm.gov/construction-industries/) and search by company name or license number. It takes 30 seconds. You'll see whether the license is active, what classifications it covers, and whether there are any complaints or disciplinary actions on file.

**Why this matters beyond legality:** if an unlicensed contractor does substandard work, you have limited recourse. The CID handles complaints against licensed contractors and can compel corrective action. With an unlicensed operator, your options are small claims court — and you have to find them first.

## Verify Their Insurance

Two types matter:

**General liability insurance** covers damage to your property during the work. If a crew member backs a skid steer into your wall or breaks a water line, the contractor's liability policy pays for the repair — not your homeowner's insurance.

**Workers' compensation insurance** covers injuries to the crew on your property. This is the one most homeowners don't think about, and it's the one that can hurt you the most. If an uninsured worker is injured on your property and the contractor doesn't carry workers' comp, you could face a claim against your homeowner's policy — or worse.

**How to verify:** ask for a certificate of insurance (COI). Any legitimate contractor can have their insurance agent send you one within a day. The COI shows coverage types, policy limits, effective dates, and names you as the certificate holder. If a contractor hesitates or says they'll "get it to you later," that's telling you something.

Don't just take their word for it. A COI is a standard document that takes five minutes to request. A contractor who pushes back on providing one either doesn't have coverage or has let it lapse.

## Get a Written Estimate

A real estimate is a document, not a conversation. It should include:

**Scope of work.** What exactly is being done? "Install patio" isn't a scope — "Remove existing grass in a 400-square-foot area, excavate to 6 inches, install 4-inch compacted aggregate base, lay concrete pavers in herringbone pattern with polymeric sand joints and aluminum edge restraint" is a scope. The more specific the description, the less room there is for misunderstandings later.

**Materials specified.** What brand and type of pavers? What aggregate for the base? What species and size of trees or shrubs? If materials aren't specified, the contractor can substitute cheaper products and you won't know until the work is done — or until it starts failing.

**Timeline.** When does work start? How long should it take? What happens if it rains (in [monsoon season](/blog/monsoon-prep-las-cruces/), this matters)? A timeline protects both of you — it sets expectations and gives you a basis for discussion if the project drags.

**Payment terms.** How much down, when are progress payments due, when is the final payment? Industry standard is a deposit (typically a percentage of the total), progress payments tied to milestones, and a final payment upon completion and your satisfaction. Never pay 100% upfront. A reasonable deposit holds your spot on the schedule; the balance keeps the contractor motivated to finish and finish right.

**Warranty.** What's covered, for how long? A contractor who stands behind their work will put the warranty in writing. Pay attention to what's excluded — plant warranties, for example, often have watering-compliance conditions.

If a contractor won't put their quote in writing, or if the written version is vague enough to mean almost anything, that's a signal. The estimate is essentially a preview of how the contractor operates. A sloppy estimate usually predicts sloppy work.

## Why the Cheapest Bid Usually Costs the Most

When you get three quotes and one is 40% below the others, the natural instinct is to wonder if the expensive ones are overcharging. Sometimes they are. But more often, the cheap bid is cheap because something has been left out.

The most common places corners get cut in Las Cruces landscape work:

**Base preparation.** This is the invisible work that determines whether hardscape stays level and drains correctly. A proper base for a [patio](/services/hardscaping/patios/) or [walkway](/services/hardscaping/walkways/) requires excavation, grading for drainage, and multiple inches of compacted aggregate. That's labor-intensive and material-heavy. A contractor who skips the base — or skimps on depth and compaction — can quote significantly less. You won't see the difference on day one. You'll see it in year two when the pavers shift, the [flagstone cracks](/blog/courtyard-rebuild-las-cruces/), or water pools against your foundation during monsoons. We covered why this matters in our [hardscape comparison guide](/blog/gravel-vs-pavers-vs-concrete-las-cruces/#the-base-matters-more-than-the-material).

**Labor.** Who actually does the work? Some contractors employ trained crews. Others hire day labor for each job. There's nothing wrong with subcontracting specialists for specific tasks — an electrician for landscape lighting, for example — but the core crew should know what they're doing and the contractor should be on site supervising. If the person who gave you the quote is never present during the work, that's worth noticing.

**Materials.** Cheaper pavers, thinner flagstone, lower-grade turf products, undersized plants. Material substitutions are the easiest way to bring a number down. This is why a detailed estimate matters — if the materials are specified, you can compare apples to apples across quotes.

**Cleanup and site restoration.** Some low bids don't include hauling debris, final grading around the work area, or restoring areas damaged by equipment access. You end up with a finished patio and a torn-up yard, and the "extra" work to fix it comes as a surprise change order.

The cheapest bid isn't always the worst choice — sometimes a smaller operation with lower overhead can genuinely do good work for less. But when a number is dramatically lower than every other quote, ask why. If the contractor can explain specifically where they're saving money without cutting quality, that's a legitimate conversation. If the answer is vague, trust the math.

## Questions Worth Asking

These aren't trick questions — they're the ones that tell you the most about whether a contractor is going to do a good job.

**"How long have you been doing this work in Las Cruces?"** Local experience matters more than general experience. Las Cruces has specific soil conditions, [drainage patterns](/services/drainage/), UV intensity, water restrictions, and plant compatibility issues that contractors from other markets won't know. Someone who's been working here for years knows what fails and why — and builds accordingly.

**"Can I see recent projects in Las Cruces?"** Not photos from a manufacturer's catalog or a website that could be stock images. Actual recent jobs, ideally that you can drive by or visit. A contractor who's proud of their work is happy to show it. A contractor who can't point to recent local work is either new to the area or doesn't have work they want you to see.

**"Who does the work — you, your crew, or subcontractors?"** There's no wrong answer here, but you should know. An owner-operator who's on site every day is a different experience than a company that dispatches crews to multiple jobs. Neither is inherently better, but the communication flow and accountability are different, and you should know what you're getting.

**"What happens if something goes wrong after the job is done?"** Listen to how they answer this one. A confident contractor describes a warranty and a process — "Call me, I'll come look at it, and if it's a workmanship issue I'll fix it." A contractor who gets evasive or defensive is telling you what the post-job relationship will look like.

**"How do you handle changes during the project?"** Change orders happen — you discover unexpected soil conditions, decide to expand the patio, or want to swap a material. A professional contractor has a process for documenting changes, adjusting the price, and getting your approval before proceeding. A contractor who "just handles it" and adjusts the final bill is a contractor you'll argue with.

**"What's your approach to drainage?"** In Las Cruces, this question is a litmus test. Any experienced local landscaper will immediately talk about grading away from the house, [monsoon runoff](/blog/monsoon-prep-las-cruces/), and how they handle water on the specific project you're discussing. If drainage isn't part of the conversation before work starts, it'll be a problem after the first heavy rain.

## Check Their Reputation Locally

Online reviews are useful but imperfect. Here's how to read them:

**Google Business Profile reviews** are the most visible and hardest to fake. Look at the content of reviews, not just the star rating. Reviews that mention specific projects, specific people, and specific outcomes are more valuable than generic "great job!" reviews. A business with 40 detailed reviews is more trustworthy than one with 200 reviews that all say the same vague thing.

**Review count matters.** A contractor who's been in business for five years and has 12 reviews is either not asking for them (possible) or not generating the kind of experience that makes people want to leave one (more concerning). A steady stream of reviews over time — not a burst followed by silence — suggests consistent work.

**How they handle negative reviews.** Every contractor eventually gets one. What matters is the response. A professional response that acknowledges the issue and describes what was done to resolve it tells you more than a perfect 5.0 that might be curated.

**BBB and trade associations.** A Better Business Bureau listing with complaints and responses gives you another data point. It's not definitive either direction, but patterns of unresolved complaints are a warning sign.

**Ask your neighbors.** In Las Cruces, word of mouth is still the most reliable signal. If three neighbors on your street used the same landscaper and are happy, that's worth more than any website.

## Timing Your Project

When you hire matters almost as much as who you hire.

**Spring (March–May)** is the busiest season. Every landscaper in Las Cruces is booked, lead times stretch to weeks, and you have less negotiating leverage on scheduling. If you want spring work, start getting quotes in January or February.

**[Fall (October–November)](/blog/fall-prep-las-cruces/)** is the best planting season in Las Cruces and a great time for landscape projects overall. Weather is cooperative, soil is workable from monsoon moisture, and schedules are more flexible than spring.

**Summer (June–September)** is split. June is brutal for outdoor work. [Monsoon season](/blog/monsoon-prep-las-cruces/) (July–September) can delay hardscape projects due to rain and wet soil. But summer is also when some contractors offer better availability — the homeowners who planned ahead are booked, and the schedule opens up. If your project can handle occasional rain delays, summer pricing and availability can work in your favor.

**Winter (December–February)** is the slowest season. Hardscape projects (concrete, pavers, flagstone) can proceed in winter without issues. Planting is limited — most trees and shrubs are dormant. But if you're [planning a landscape project](/blog/planning-landscape-project-las-cruces/) for spring, winter is when you should be getting quotes, comparing contractors, and locking in your spot on the schedule.

## The Short Version

Before you sign anything, verify the license, verify the insurance, and get a detailed written estimate. Ask the questions above, and pay attention to how the contractor communicates — responsiveness and clarity before the job predict responsiveness and clarity during it. Check their recent local work, not just their website.

The best landscaper for your project is the one who shows up, listens to what you want, gives you honest feedback about what will and won't work in your specific yard, puts it in writing, and then does what they said they'd do. That's a lower bar than it sounds — but it's the one that matters.
