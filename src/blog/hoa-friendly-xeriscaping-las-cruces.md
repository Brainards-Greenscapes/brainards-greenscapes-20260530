If you live in one of Las Cruces's HOA neighborhoods — Sonoma Ranch, Picacho Hills, the Metro Verde subdivisions, or any of the newer developments on the east mesa — you've probably thought about [xeriscaping](/services/landscaping/xeriscaping/). The water savings alone make it worth considering. But then there's the HOA.

The question most homeowners ask is: "Can my HOA stop me from xeriscaping?" The answer is more nuanced than you'd expect, and getting it wrong can mean an expensive redo or a violation you have to fight.

## New Mexico Law Won't Save You

This needs to be said upfront because there's bad information circulating online. Some websites claim that New Mexico has a state law (often citing a nonexistent statute at NMSA 47-16-7.1) that prevents HOAs from banning water-efficient landscaping. **That statute does not exist.** The New Mexico Homeowner Association Act (NMSA 47-16) covers board duties, assessments, meetings, and governance — but it contains no provision about landscaping, xeriscaping, or water conservation.

Unlike Arizona, Colorado, and Nevada — which have explicit state laws protecting homeowners' right to install water-efficient landscaping — New Mexico leaves this entirely to your HOA's governing documents. Your CC&Rs (Covenants, Conditions & Restrictions) and any architectural or landscape design guidelines are the complete rulebook for what you can and can't do with your yard.

That doesn't mean you can't xeriscape. It means you need to work within your HOA's process rather than assuming you have a legal right to override it.

## Read Your Guidelines First

Before you plan anything, get a copy of your CC&Rs and your HOA's architectural guidelines. These are usually available from your management company or on your HOA's website. You're looking for:

**Landscape-specific rules.** Some HOAs have detailed landscape design standards. Others have a single paragraph that says "yards must be maintained in good condition." The more specific the rules, the clearer your path — and the easier it is to design a xeriscape that explicitly complies.

**Minimum living plant coverage.** Some HOAs require a minimum percentage of living plant material in the front yard — often 40–60%. This doesn't mean grass; shrubs, groundcovers, and trees all count. A yard that's 90% gravel with one cactus may not meet this requirement, but a thoughtfully planted xeriscape with desert shrubs, ornamental grasses, and groundcovers typically will.

**Approved materials and colors.** Many HOAs specify acceptable gravel or rock colors (tan and earth tones are usually fine; bright white or red river rock may not be), and some restrict the types of edging, borders, or decorative elements you can use.

**Irrigation requirements.** Some HOAs require a functioning irrigation system regardless of landscape type. This is actually fine for xeriscaping — a [drip irrigation system](/services/irrigation/drip-irrigation/) is the right way to water a xeriscape anyway, and it satisfies the requirement while using a fraction of the water a spray system needs.

**Restrictions on artificial turf.** If you're considering combining [artificial turf](/services/landscaping/artificial-turf/) with xeriscape (a popular combination), check whether your HOA has specific rules about turf products, pile height, or placement.

**The approval process itself.** Most HOAs require you to submit plans to an Architectural Review Committee (ARC) before making exterior changes. Doing the work first and asking permission later is the fastest way to get a violation notice and an order to restore your yard at your own expense.

## The ARC Approval Process

The ARC (sometimes called the ACC — Architectural Control Committee) reviews exterior changes to make sure they meet the community's standards. For a xeriscape conversion, your submission should include:

**A site plan.** This doesn't need to be an architect's blueprint, but it should show your property boundaries, your house footprint, existing trees you're keeping, and the layout of what you're proposing — where gravel goes, where planting beds go, where hardscape features go, where the irrigation will run.

**A plant list.** Name each species you're planting, how many of each, and where they go on the plan. This demonstrates that you've thought through the design rather than just planning to dump gravel and call it done. Our [desert plants guide](/blog/desert-plants-las-cruces/) covers species that do well in Las Cruces, and most HOAs respond well to a list of established, commercially available plants rather than obscure species they've never heard of.

**Material samples or photos.** What color and type of gravel or decomposed granite? What kind of edging? If you're including pavers, [flagstone](/services/hardscaping/patios/), or other hardscape, show the material. ARCs respond to visuals. A color photo of the rock you're proposing, next to a photo of your house's exterior, shows them exactly what the finished product will look like next to the existing architecture.

**Photos of similar completed projects.** If your landscaper has a [portfolio](/projects/) of finished xeriscape work, include photos of completed projects that have a similar aesthetic to what you're proposing. This is one of the most effective things you can include — it turns an abstract plan into something the committee can visualize.

**A maintenance plan.** One sentence is fine: "Drip irrigation on a timer, pre-emergent weed treatment in spring and fall, seasonal pruning, and gravel raking as needed." This tells the ARC you're not going to install it and forget it.

Most ARCs respond within 2–6 weeks. A complete, professional-looking submission moves faster than a vague one that generates questions.

## Design Principles That Pass Review

The difference between a xeriscape that gets approved and one that gets denied usually isn't the concept — it's the execution. HOAs are trying to maintain property values and neighborhood appearance. A xeriscape that looks intentional, well-designed, and maintained is easy to approve. One that looks like the homeowner just stopped watering is not.

**Clean edges define the space.** Steel or aluminum landscape edging between gravel and planting beds, between different ground materials, and along walkways creates the crisp lines that signal "this was designed." Without edging, gravel migrates into beds, beds creep into gravel, and within a season the whole thing looks unfinished. [Stone borders](/services/hardscaping/stone-borders/) are another option that adds visual structure.

**Layer your heights.** A flat field of gravel with a few scattered shrubs reads as empty. A design that layers groundcovers in front, mid-height shrubs in the middle, and a tree or tall accent plant in the back creates depth and visual interest. This is basic landscape design, but it's the thing most DIY xeriscapes miss — and it's the thing that makes an ARC say "that looks great" instead of "that looks bare."

**Include focal points.** A boulder grouping, a specimen plant like a Desert Museum Palo Verde, a decorative pot, or a small flagstone seating area gives the eye something to land on. A front yard xeriscape without a focal point feels like a vacant lot with some plants in it.

**Use color strategically.** Desert plants aren't all gray-green. Texas Sage blooms purple after rain. Red Yucca sends up coral flower stalks from spring through fall. Autumn Sage blooms red most of the year. Penstemon, Desert Marigold, and Blackfoot Daisy provide seasonal color without the water demand of annual flower beds. Mixing bloom times means something is always showing color, which keeps the yard looking alive and intentional year-round.

**Don't go 100% gravel.** This is the single biggest mistake in HOA xeriscaping. A front yard that's wall-to-wall [gravel or decomposed granite](/services/landscaping/gravel-rock/) with a few plants looks like a construction staging area, not a landscape. Aim for the plant-coverage percentage your HOA requires at minimum — and ideally exceed it. More plants, not fewer, is what makes a xeriscape look like a landscape rather than a vacant lot.

**Match the neighborhood context.** Look at what other homes in your subdivision have done. If the street is mostly traditional landscapes and you propose a radically different aesthetic, expect more pushback. If several neighbors already have xeriscape or desert-adapted landscapes, your proposal has built-in precedent.

## Common HOA Concerns (And How to Address Them)

**"It'll look like a rock yard."** This is the most common objection. Address it with your design: show the plant coverage percentage, the layered planting plan, the color variety, and photos of completed projects. The word "xeriscape" sometimes triggers a mental image of bare gravel with a single cactus. Your submission should make it impossible to picture that.

**"It'll attract weeds."** Pre-emergent herbicide in spring and fall, landscape fabric under gravel areas, and drip irrigation that waters plants (not bare ground) controls weeds effectively. Include this in your maintenance plan.

**"Property values."** In Las Cruces, a well-designed xeriscape is a selling point, not a liability. [Water costs are climbing](/blog/xeriscaping-water-savings-las-cruces/), drought awareness is increasing, and buyers in the Mesilla Valley actively look for low-water landscaping. A maintained xeriscape that matches the desert aesthetic adds value.

**"It doesn't match the neighborhood."** If your neighborhood already has xeriscaped homes, point to them. If it doesn't, frame your proposal as an upgrade rather than a departure — you're investing in professional landscape design, not abandoning maintenance.

## Plants That Look Intentional

The plant selection is what separates a landscape from a rock garden. Here are groupings that work well in Las Cruces HOA neighborhoods because they look maintained, bloom reliably, and stay structured:

**Foundation plantings** (along the house): Compact Texas Sage, Autumn Sage, Damianita. These stay dense, bloom regularly, and have a groomed appearance that reads as "landscaped."

**Mid-bed structure** (middle layer): Red Yucca, Desert Spoon, Agave, Mexican Feather Grass. These provide year-round architectural form — they look good even when nothing is blooming.

**Groundcovers** (front of beds, between larger plants): Blackfoot Daisy, Desert Zinnia, trailing Lantana. These fill gaps, soften edges, and prevent the "plants floating in gravel" look that triggers HOA concerns.

**Accent trees** (one or two per yard): Desert Museum Palo Verde, Chilean Mesquite, Desert Willow. These provide shade, vertical structure, and the sense of a "finished" landscape.

## Maintenance Isn't Optional

Xeriscaping reduces maintenance compared to a grass lawn. It doesn't eliminate it. And HOAs care about upkeep — a neglected xeriscape with weeds growing through gravel, dead plants left standing, and gravel scattered onto the sidewalk will generate violations faster than a brown lawn.

**What ongoing maintenance looks like:**

Weed control — pre-emergent treatments in February and September, spot-pulling or spot-spraying between. Drip irrigation on a timer handles watering with minimal intervention, but check emitters seasonally for clogs ([hard water is real here](/blog/irrigation-problems-las-cruces/#hard-water-and-clogged-emitters)). Prune shrubs and remove spent flower stalks once or twice a year. Rake gravel back into place after monsoon storms push it around. Remove dead plants and replace them — a gap in the planting bed sends the same signal as a dead patch in a lawn.

This is a fraction of the work a grass lawn requires. No mowing, no fertilizing, no aeration, no overseeding, no adjusting sprinklers to the [watering schedule](/blog/summer-survival-guide-las-cruces-landscape/#the-watering-schedule-you-have-to-follow). But it's not zero, and being honest about that with your HOA — and with yourself — is what keeps the yard looking good and the violation letters away.
