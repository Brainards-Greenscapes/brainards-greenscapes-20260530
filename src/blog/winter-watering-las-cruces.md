Most Las Cruces irrigation timers get set once, in May or June, and never touched again. That works fine through the summer. By December, the same program is putting down water your landscape has no use for. The plants don't benefit, the extra water runs off or sits in cold soil, and you pay for all of it.

Winter is the easiest season to save water in the Mesilla Valley. You don't need new equipment or new plants. You just need to know how much less your yard actually needs, and adjust.

## How Much Less Water Your Yard Needs in Winter

Plants lose water through their leaves and the soil loses it to evaporation. Together that's called evapotranspiration, or ET, and it's the best single measure of how much water your landscape needs to replace.

In Las Cruces, ET collapses in winter. NMSU's turfgrass irrigation guide puts the average daily water loss for cool-season grass in Las Cruces at about **0.01 inches a day in early January**, rising only to around 0.05 inches by late February ([Guide H-504](https://pubs.nmsu.edu/_h/H504/)). At the June peak, the same lawn loses about 0.27 inches a day. As we covered in our [smart controller guide](/blog/smart-irrigation-controllers-las-cruces/#the-problem-with-fixed-schedule-timers), that's more than a 25-fold swing between the hottest and coldest parts of the year.

Trees and shrubs follow the same pattern. Deciduous plants have dropped their leaves, their metabolism has slowed, and cold temperatures cut water loss even further. Evergreens keep their leaves and keep using some water, but still far less than in summer ([NMSU Southwest Yard & Garden](https://swyg.nmsu.edu/2009/121909.html)).

The takeaway: if your watering schedule looks the same in December as it did in July, you're applying many times more water than your landscape can use.

## Same Depth, Less Often

The most important rule of winter watering is also the one people get backwards. **Don't make each watering shorter. Make the waterings farther apart.**

NMSU Extension's guidance is to keep watering depth constant year-round and only change frequency ([Southwest Yard & Garden, 2019](https://swyg.nmsu.edu/2019/112319.html)):

- **Trees:** wet the soil 24–36 inches deep
- **Shrubs:** 18–24 inches
- **Groundcovers and vines:** 8–12 inches
- **Turf:** 6–10 inches

Short, shallow winter waterings only wet the top few inches. Roots deeper down dry out, and a deep root system is exactly what carries desert plants through a Las Cruces summer. One thorough soak every several weeks does more good than a light sprinkle every few days, and it uses less water overall.

**For drip systems,** that usually means leaving each zone's run time alone and cutting the number of watering days. For sprinkler zones, use the controller's seasonal adjust (often a percentage setting) carefully: if it shortens every run, you may be making waterings shallower instead of less frequent. Switching to fewer watering days is the cleaner fix.

## A Winter Watering Schedule by Plant Type

These guidelines come from NMSU Extension, drawing on the *Arizona & New Mexico Getting Started Garden Guide* by Mary Irish and Judith Phillips. They apply to **established, desert-adapted plants** (in the ground more than a year, or more than three years for trees) for **December through March** ([NMSU, 2017](https://swyg.nmsu.edu/2017/120917.html)):

| Plant type | Winter watering frequency |
|---|---|
| Trees and shrubs | Every 45–60 days |
| Warm-season grass (bermuda, dormant) | Every 45–60 days |
| Groundcovers and vines | Every 30–60 days |
| Cool-season grass (fescue, bluegrass) | About every 30 days |
| Cacti and succulents | Often none |
| Annuals in bloom | Every 10–14 days |

Compare that to the growing season, when NMSU suggests watering trees and perennials about every 10 days to two weeks ([NMSU, 1998](https://swyg.nmsu.edu/1998/011998.html)). For most established desert trees and shrubs, a winter schedule means roughly a quarter as many waterings, or fewer.

A few Las Cruces specifics:

**Dormant bermuda doesn't need regular water.** NMSU's turfgrass specialist notes that a brown bermuda lawn is dormant, not dead, and doesn't need routine winter irrigation. A monthly watering will help it green up a little earlier in spring, but it isn't required ([NMSU, 2008](https://swyg.nmsu.edu/2008/110808.html)).

**Cool-season lawns need more.** If you have fescue or bluegrass that stays green through winter, NMSU recommends roughly once a week in November and February and every other week in December and January in a dry winter, adjusting for any rain or snow ([NMSU, 2008](https://swyg.nmsu.edu/2008/110808.html)). It's also one reason a cool-season lawn is worth reconsidering in favor of [artificial turf](/services/landscaping/artificial-turf/) or [xeriscaping](/services/landscaping/xeriscaping/).

**Some natives want to be left alone.** Plants native to the lower elevations of New Mexico rarely need winter irrigation, and NMSU notes that desert willow does best with none at all ([NMSU, 2014](https://swyg.nmsu.edu/2014/021514.html)). Most cacti and succulents are in the same camp. Cold, wet soil is harder on them than a dry winter.

**Rain and snow help, but rarely replace a watering.** About 10 inches of snow equals roughly one inch of water, so a typical Las Cruces dusting won't reach deep roots ([NMSU, 2019](https://swyg.nmsu.edu/2019/112319.html)). Skip a scheduled watering after a genuinely soaking storm; otherwise, stay on schedule.

### Check Before You Water

The simplest moisture test is a long screwdriver or a piece of rebar. Push it into the soil near the plant's drip line. It slides easily through moist soil and stops where the soil is dry ([NMSU, 2019](https://swyg.nmsu.edu/2019/112319.html)). If it goes down a foot or more without resistance, the root zone is still wet and you can wait.

One caution: in some Las Cruces neighborhoods, the rod can also stop on a hard [caliche layer](/blog/caliche-las-cruces/) rather than dry soil. If you hit something hard at the same shallow depth every time, that's worth knowing about before your next planting project.

## The Money Part: Winter Water Sets Your Sewer Bill

Here's the part most homeowners don't know. If Las Cruces Utilities handles your wastewater, your sewer charge isn't metered at your drain. LCU estimates it from your winter water use.

The water you use from November through February shows up on your December, January, and February bills. LCU averages those three bills, takes 90% of that average, and uses the result as your sewer billing volume starting in March, for the next year ([City of Las Cruces](https://lascruces.gov/?p=62946)). LCU's reasoning is that landscape watering should be very low or nonexistent in winter, so winter use is a good stand-in for what actually goes down your drains ([KRWG](https://www.krwg.org/regional/2018-10-28/how-to-reduce-your-wastewater-bill-in-las-cruces)).

That means **every unnecessary gallon you put on the yard this winter gets counted twice**: once on the water bill now, and again in your sewer charge every month for the following year. Cutting winter irrigation back to what your plants actually need is one of the few things you can do in a few minutes at the controller that keeps saving money for 12 months.

Two related things worth checking:

- **Fix leaks before December.** A running toilet or a weeping irrigation valve during the winter quarter inflates your sewer average the same way overwatering does. Our [irrigation problems guide](/blog/irrigation-problems-las-cruces/#mystery-water-bill-the-leak-test) walks through a simple meter leak test.
- **On another water provider?** If your water comes from Moongate, DAMDWCA, or another system but LCU provides your sewer, LCU can calculate your sewer charge from your water provider's bills ([City of Las Cruces](https://lascruces.gov/?p=62946)), so the same winter habits apply.

**A quick note on watering days:** LCU's odd/even watering days still apply from October 1 through March 31, but the summer ban on daytime watering is lifted ([City of Las Cruces](https://lascruces.gov/fall-winter-outdoor-watering-restrictions)). That's helpful, because midday is the best time to water in winter.

## When to Water: Warm Days, Midday

Winter timing is the opposite of summer. Instead of watering before dawn, water on warmer days when temperatures are above freezing, ideally around midday:

- **Don't water frozen ground.** Ice blocks water from soaking in, so it runs off instead of reaching roots ([NMSU, 2009](https://swyg.nmsu.edu/2009/121909.html)).
- **Avoid cold-night watering.** Water left standing on walks and driveways overnight freezes into a slip hazard.
- **Disconnect hoses before dark.** A hose left on an outdoor faucet on a freezing night can crack the pipe ([NMSU, 2009](https://swyg.nmsu.edu/2009/121909.html)).

If you have a [smart controller](/services/irrigation/smart-controllers/), it already sees how little water your landscape is losing and cuts back on its own. Just confirm its start times have shifted out of the early-morning summer window.

## The Exceptions: What Still Needs Regular Water

Cutting back doesn't mean shutting off. A few parts of your landscape still need attention:

**Anything planted this fall.** Fall is the [best planting season in Las Cruces](/blog/fall-prep-las-cruces/), but new transplants don't have established roots yet. NMSU notes that plants installed in the past summer or fall need more frequent winter watering than the schedule above, even if they're desert-adapted ([NMSU, 2019](https://swyg.nmsu.edu/2019/112319.html)). See our [first-year tree care section](/blog/best-trees-las-cruces/#first-year-care-the-make-or-break-period) for more.

**Evergreens.** Pines, junipers, and broadleaf evergreens keep their leaves and keep losing water through the winter, so they need more than deciduous plants. Broadleaf evergreens need the most ([NMSU, 2014](https://swyg.nmsu.edu/2014/021514.html)).

**Trees in late winter.** January and February are often dry, and late winter is when trees begin building new roots as their buds swell. NMSU calls this one of the most important times to water trees if there's been no rain ([NMSU, 2009](https://swyg.nmsu.edu/2009/121909.html)). If you water a tree once all winter, make it a deep soak in mid-to-late February.

## Frost: A Quick Word

Las Cruces is USDA Zone 8a, with winter lows in the 10–15°F range in a typical year. The [desert-adapted plants](/blog/desert-plants-las-cruces/) and [trees](/blog/best-trees-las-cruces/) we recommend handle that without help. For anything marginal, like young or potted plants, or tender species near the edge of their range:

- Cover them before sunset on a hard-freeze night with frost cloth or an old sheet, draped all the way to the ground. Avoid plastic touching the leaves.
- Remove covers the next morning once temperatures climb above freezing.
- Move potted plants against a south-facing wall, or into the garage. Roots in containers are much less insulated than roots in the ground.

Lightweight frost cloth typically buys a few degrees of protection, roughly 2–7°F depending on weight ([NMSU Guide H-251](https://pubs.nmsu.edu/_h/H251/)). That's enough for a cold snap, not a substitute for choosing plants that belong here.

## The Short Version

- Your landscape needs a small fraction of its summer water from December through February.
- Keep each watering deep, but space waterings weeks apart. Most established desert trees, shrubs, and dormant bermuda need water every 45–60 days.
- Water on warm days, around midday, and never on frozen ground.
- Give extra attention to fall plantings, evergreens, and trees in late February.
- If LCU handles your sewer, winter overwatering raises your sewer charge for the next 12 months. Cutting back now pays off all year.
