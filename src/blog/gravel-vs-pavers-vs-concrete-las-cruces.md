If you're planning a [patio](/services/hardscaping/patios/), [walkway](/services/hardscaping/walkways/), or [driveway](/services/hardscaping/driveways/) in Las Cruces, the first decision is what to build it out of. And the honest answer is that there's no single best material — just trade-offs that matter more or less depending on your project, your yard, and how you use the space.

This isn't a ranking. It's a comparison based on how each material actually performs in Las Cruces conditions — the heat, the soil, the monsoons, and the UV. We install all of these, and we'll tell you the same thing on a job site that we'll tell you here: each one has situations where it's the right choice and situations where it isn't.

## Heat: How Hot Does It Get Underfoot?

In a climate where summer surface temperatures routinely exceed 140°F, the material you walk on matters — especially for patios and pool surrounds where bare feet are common.

**Poured concrete** absorbs solar radiation efficiently and retains it. A south-facing concrete slab in full Las Cruces sun can hit 150°F or higher by mid-afternoon in June. Dark-colored stamped concrete runs even hotter. It stays hot well into the evening because concrete's thermal mass releases heat slowly.

**Concrete pavers** behave similarly to poured concrete — they're made of the same material. Dark-colored pavers absorb more heat. Light-colored or tumbled pavers run somewhat cooler, but the difference between concrete pavers and poured concrete is modest.

**Flagstone and natural stone** vary by type and color, but natural stone generally runs cooler than concrete products. Light-colored sandstone and limestone reflect more solar radiation and have lower thermal mass. A tan or buff flagstone patio will be noticeably more comfortable underfoot than a gray concrete slab in the same sun exposure.

**Gravel and decomposed granite** are the coolest options. The loose surface doesn't store heat the way a solid mass does, and air circulates between the particles. Light-colored gravel — tan, buff, or desert gold — stays the most comfortable. Dark river rock gets hot, though not as hot as solid concrete.

**The practical takeaway:** if your patio gets full afternoon sun and you want to use it barefoot in summer, lighter-colored natural stone or gravel are the most comfortable choices. If the area is shaded or you'll always wear shoes, heat retention is less of a factor and you can choose based on other criteria.

## Durability on Las Cruces Soil

This is where Las Cruces conditions diverge from what you'll read in most hardscaping guides. Our soil moves. [Caliche](/blog/caliche-las-cruces/) creates an impermeable layer that traps moisture above it during monsoons, and that moisture causes the upper soil to expand. When it dries out — which happens fast in our climate — it contracts. This seasonal expansion-contraction cycle puts stress on any rigid surface sitting on top of it.

**Poured concrete** is rigid. It can't flex with soil movement, so it cracks. On stable, well-drained soil with no caliche, a properly poured slab with control joints can last decades. On Las Cruces caliche soils, cracking is common within a few years — especially on larger slabs. Once a poured slab cracks, the repair is visible. You can fill the crack, but you can't make it disappear. Stamped concrete is worse in this regard because the decorative pattern makes any crack more obvious and harder to patch convincingly.

**Concrete pavers** are individual units set on a compacted base with sand joints. When the soil shifts, the pavers shift with it — slightly, individually — instead of cracking. If a paver settles, heaves, or breaks, you pull it up and replace it. The rest of the surface stays intact. This is why [pavers](/services/hardscaping/pavers/) are often the better choice on Las Cruces soil for large patio surfaces and driveways — they tolerate ground movement that would crack a poured slab.

**Flagstone** shares this advantage. Individual stones set on a compacted base flex with soil movement. If a stone cracks (usually from a point load, not soil movement), you replace that stone. We just did exactly this on a [courtyard patio rebuild](/blog/courtyard-rebuild-las-cruces/) where the original flagstone had been damaged — individual stones came out and new ones went in without disturbing the rest of the surface.

**Gravel and decomposed granite** are inherently flexible — there's nothing rigid to crack. Soil movement under a gravel surface is invisible. The trade-off is that gravel migrates over time (especially on slopes) and needs periodic raking and replenishment. But it will never crack, heave, or require structural repair.

**The practical takeaway:** on Las Cruces caliche soils, flexible systems (pavers, flagstone, gravel) generally outperform rigid systems (poured concrete) for long-term durability. If you want poured concrete, invest in a thicker base, more control joints, and fiber reinforcement — and accept that some cracking is likely over time.

## Drainage and Monsoon Performance

Las Cruces gets roughly half its annual rainfall — about 4.5 to 5 inches — during [monsoon season](/blog/monsoon-prep-las-cruces/) in short, intense bursts. An inch of rain in 30 minutes is normal. That's a lot of water hitting your hardscape surface very fast, and where it goes depends entirely on what the surface is.

**Poured concrete** is impermeable. Every drop that hits the slab runs to the edges. If your patio slopes toward the house instead of away from it, you've got water pooling against the foundation — the exact problem we talked about in our [monsoon prep guide](/blog/monsoon-prep-las-cruces/#check-your-grading-first). On a large concrete driveway, the runoff volume during a heavy monsoon storm is significant, and it all concentrates at the low point.

**Pavers with sand joints** are semi-permeable. Some water infiltrates through the joints, reducing total runoff. The drainage benefit varies by joint width and sand type, but it's meaningful — especially compared to a sealed concrete surface. Pavers with polymeric sand joints drain less than those with regular sand, but they also resist weed growth and ant infestations.

**Flagstone with gaps** works similarly to pavers — water percolates through the joints. Dry-laid flagstone (set on a gravel base without mortar) is the most permeable option among solid hardscapes. Mortared flagstone drains more like poured concrete.

**Gravel and decomposed granite** drain the best. Water passes through the surface and into the base material. In a monsoon burst, gravel doesn't generate the sheet runoff that concrete does. However, on a slope, loose gravel can wash downhill during heavy rain. Stabilized decomposed granite (DG mixed with a binding agent) holds up better on slopes while still draining well.

**The practical takeaway:** in Las Cruces, drainage isn't optional — it's the first thing David checks on any hardscape project. Whatever material you choose, the surface has to slope away from structures, and the water has to have somewhere to go. Permeable surfaces (gravel, gapped pavers) give you more margin for error. Impermeable surfaces (concrete) require more deliberate grading and sometimes [drainage infrastructure](/services/drainage/) to handle monsoon volumes.

## Maintenance: What Each Surface Actually Needs

**Gravel and decomposed granite** need periodic attention but not much skill. Rake it level a few times a year. Add fresh material every year or two to replace what's been scattered by wind, foot traffic, or monsoon wash. Pull weeds that sprout through (or apply pre-emergent in early spring). Stabilized DG needs less replenishment but can't be raked without breaking the surface bond.

**Concrete pavers** need almost nothing once installed. Sweep or blow off debris. If a paver settles, lift it, add base material, and reset it — a 15-minute job. If weeds grow in sand joints, pull them or spot-spray. Polymeric sand joints resist weeds for several years before needing reapplication. UV doesn't degrade pavers the way it degrades plastic or rubber.

**Flagstone** maintains similarly to pavers. Sweep, occasional weed management in joints, and individual stone replacement if something cracks. Mortared flagstone joints can crack and need regrouting over time — the mortar is typically the first thing that fails, not the stone itself.

**Poured concrete** is genuinely zero-maintenance — until it's not. There's nothing to rake, replenish, or reset. But when a slab cracks or a section settles, the fix is significant: you're looking at mudjacking, slab replacement, or grinding, and none of those are invisible. Sealed concrete needs resealing every few years to maintain appearance and resist staining. Stamped concrete is the highest-maintenance concrete option because the color sealer degrades under UV and needs periodic recoating.

**The practical takeaway:** there's a difference between low-maintenance and no-maintenance. Gravel needs regular small effort. Pavers and flagstone need occasional small effort. Concrete needs no effort until it needs a big effort. Choose based on whether you'd rather do a little regularly or deal with a larger project less often.

## UV and Longevity

Las Cruces gets over 300 days of sunshine a year. UV exposure affects hardscape materials differently:

**Natural stone** (flagstone, sandstone, limestone) handles UV indefinitely. Stone that's been in desert sun for a thousand years looks fine. Color may fade slightly over decades, but the material itself doesn't degrade. This is the most UV-durable hardscape option.

**Concrete** (poured and pavers) doesn't degrade structurally from UV, but surface treatments do. Stamped concrete color sealers fade and peel. Integral-color concrete holds up better but still fades over time. Unstained gray concrete looks about the same after 20 years as it did after 1.

**Gravel** is UV-proof. It's rock. It doesn't fade, crack, or degrade from sun exposure. Stabilized DG binding agents can break down under UV over several years and may need reapplication, but the aggregate itself is permanent.

## Which Material for Which Job

Rather than ranking materials overall, here's where each one tends to make the most sense in Las Cruces:

### Patios

**Best options:** Flagstone (for natural look and heat comfort) or pavers (for design flexibility and consistent surface). Both handle soil movement well and are individually repairable.

**When concrete makes sense:** Smaller slabs in shaded areas, or when you want a specific stamped pattern and understand the cracking trade-off. Budget-conscious projects where the square footage is large.

**When gravel makes sense:** Casual outdoor areas, fire pit surrounds, or spaces where you want a rustic desert feel. Not ideal for dining areas (furniture doesn't sit level on loose gravel) unless you use stabilized DG.

### Walkways

**Best options:** Flagstone for front-of-house paths (looks great, drains well, stays cool). Pavers for high-traffic side yards and utility paths (consistent surface height, easy to snowblow if you're in the east foothills).

**When concrete makes sense:** ADA-accessible paths that need a perfectly smooth, continuous surface.

**When gravel makes sense:** Garden paths, backyard circulation, and secondary walkways where the natural look fits. [Landscape rock](/services/landscaping/gravel-rock/) borders keep the gravel from migrating into plant beds.

### Driveways

**Best options:** Pavers for the highest durability and individual-unit repairability under vehicle loads. They distribute weight across the base without concentrating stress at any single point.

**When concrete makes sense:** Standard residential driveways where budget matters and you're willing to accept control-joint cracking over time. Concrete driveways are common in Las Cruces and work fine with proper base prep and joints — they're just harder to repair invisibly when they do crack.

**When gravel makes sense:** Long rural driveways where paving the entire length isn't practical. Compacted road base or crusher fines create a drivable surface that drains well and costs a fraction of paved options. Needs grading and replenishment periodically.

## The Base Matters More Than the Material

This is the point we come back to on every hardscape project, and it's the most important takeaway from this comparison: in Las Cruces, the base preparation underneath your hardscape determines its longevity more than the surface material you choose.

On [caliche soils](/blog/caliche-las-cruces/), a properly compacted aggregate base serves two functions: it distributes weight evenly so the surface doesn't develop low spots, and it creates a stable layer between the surface and the expansive soil underneath. Without that base, any material — flagstone, pavers, concrete, gravel — will shift, settle, or crack as the soil beneath it moves with seasonal moisture changes.

We covered this in detail during the [courtyard rebuild](/blog/courtyard-rebuild-las-cruces/#rebuilding-from-the-ground-up), where base preparation took more time than the stone-setting itself. That's normal. On every hardscape project David quotes, the base work is the largest portion of the labor — and it's the part that determines whether you're happy with the surface five years from now.

A beautiful flagstone patio on a poor base will fail. A simple gravel pad on a properly compacted base will look good for years. The material is visible; the base is invisible. But the base is what you're really paying for.
