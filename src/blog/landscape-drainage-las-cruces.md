Monsoon season in Las Cruces is a yearly reminder of which yards drain and which ones don't. A storm drops an inch of rain in 20 minutes, and suddenly there's standing water against the foundation, a lake in the backyard, gravel washed into the driveway, and the side yard is a muddy channel that takes days to dry out.

Some homeowners assume that's just how desert landscaping works — the ground is hard, rain comes fast, and water has to go somewhere. But standing water in your yard isn't a feature of the desert. It's a sign that something in your drainage isn't working, and in Las Cruces, the usual suspects are specific and fixable.

## The Drainage Equation in Las Cruces

Water that falls on your property needs to do one of three things: soak into the ground, flow across the surface to a lower point, or evaporate. In a well-drained yard, most water from a typical storm infiltrates the soil within an hour or two, and surface flow moves away from structures and toward the street, an arroyo, or a designated low area.

When drainage fails, it's because one or more of those pathways is blocked. In Las Cruces, the blocks are predictable:

**The ground won't absorb it.** Compacted desert soil, especially after construction, has very low infiltration rates. Add a layer of [caliche](/blog/what-is-caliche-las-cruces/) underneath and water has nowhere to go vertically.

**The surface won't move it.** Flat grading, or grading that slopes toward the house instead of away from it, traps water against foundations and in low spots. This is common in newer subdivisions where the builder graded for the structure but didn't think much about the landscape.

**There's no outlet.** Walls, fences, raised garden beds, and [hardscape features](/blog/gravel-vs-pavers-vs-concrete-las-cruces/) can create dams that trap water in areas that used to drain freely. Even a well-intentioned landscape renovation can create new drainage problems if runoff paths aren't part of the design.

## Why Las Cruces Drainage Is Different

Every landscaping market has drainage challenges, but ours are specific to the Mesilla Valley geology. Understanding them determines which solutions work and which ones are a waste of money.

### Caliche: The Underground Dam

[Caliche](/blog/what-is-caliche-las-cruces/) is a layer of calcium carbonate hardpan that sits under much of the Las Cruces metro area. It varies from a few inches to several feet thick, and from a soft chalky layer to something that requires a jackhammer to break through. The depth varies too — some lots have it six inches down, others at three feet, and some properties have multiple layers.

From a drainage perspective, caliche acts like an impermeable floor. Water that soaks through the top few inches of soil hits the caliche and stops. It pools on top of the hardpan, saturating the root zone above it. This is why trees and shrubs planted above intact caliche often develop root rot even in the desert — the water has nowhere to go.

Any drainage solution for a Las Cruces property needs to account for caliche. A French drain trenched into soil above an intact caliche layer is just a longer-shaped pool. A dry well dug to a depth that doesn't break through caliche is an underground water tank. You have to either punch through the caliche to reach drainable soil below, or route water laterally to a point where it can discharge.

### Alkaline Soil Structure

Our soil pH of [7.5–8.5](/blog/wind-sun-soil-mesilla-valley-landscaping/) contributes to poor soil structure. High-pH soils with elevated sodium levels (common near the Rio Grande) tend to have dispersed clay particles that seal the soil surface, reducing infiltration. After a monsoon storm, you can see this as a hard crust on bare soil — water beads on it instead of soaking in.

### Monsoon Intensity

Las Cruces monsoon storms are short and intense. An inch of rain in 20–30 minutes is common from July through September. That rate overwhelms any soil's infiltration capacity, so surface runoff is a given during active storms. The question isn't whether water will flow across your property during a monsoon — it will. The question is whether it flows where you want it to, and whether it drains within a reasonable time after the storm passes.

## Solution 1: Regrading

Regrading is the most cost-effective drainage fix and the one that gets overlooked most often. Many drainage problems aren't caused by soil conditions at all — they're caused by the ground being flat or sloping the wrong direction.

**What regrading does:** It reshapes the soil surface so water flows away from structures and toward designated drainage points — the street, an arroyo, a swale, or a dry well.

**The standard:** For residential properties, a minimum slope of 2% (roughly ¼ inch per foot) away from the foundation for the first 6–10 feet. Beyond that, the grade should continue directing water toward the property's lowest point or discharge area.

**When it works best:** Regrading handles surface water accumulation — the water that pools against the house, in the middle of the yard, or in low spots that collect runoff from higher ground. It's particularly effective for properties where the original builder grade has settled, where fill dirt has compacted, or where landscape additions have inadvertently leveled out the slope.

**Limitations:** Regrading doesn't solve subsurface drainage problems. If water is infiltrating the soil and pooling on top of caliche underground, re-sloping the surface won't help with that. You need a subsurface solution.

## Solution 2: French Drains

A French drain is a gravel-filled trench with a perforated pipe that collects and redirects subsurface water. It's the standard solution for water that's pooling underground rather than on the surface.

**How it works in Las Cruces:** The trench intercepts water moving through the soil above the caliche layer and redirects it — via gravity — to a discharge point at a lower elevation. The perforated pipe sits in gravel that acts as a collection channel, pulling water from the surrounding saturated soil.

**The caliche complication:** A French drain installed above an intact caliche layer works only if the trench slopes to a discharge point that's lower than the bottom of the trench. If there's nowhere for the pipe to drain to — if the trench dead-ends in your yard — the water just fills the trench and sits there. You've built an underground pool.

The solutions for this are:

**Drainage chimneys.** Every 3–4 feet along the French drain, drill 4–6 inch diameter holes through the caliche layer into the drainable soil or decomposed rock below. Fill these vertical holes with gravel. Water from the French drain percolates down through the chimneys, bypassing the caliche. This only works if the soil below the caliche drains well — if there's another caliche layer below, you're back to the same problem.

**Gravity discharge.** Route the French drain pipe (solid, not perforated, for the discharge run) to a point at a lower elevation where it can daylight — a slope, an arroyo, or the street. The trench needs a consistent downhill slope of at least 1% to maintain flow. This works well on properties with any significant grade change.

**Sump and pump.** For flat properties where gravity discharge isn't possible and caliche is too deep or thick to drill through, a sump basin at the low end of the French drain with a small pump can move collected water to the surface or to a discharge point. This is the most expensive option and requires electricity and maintenance, but it works when nothing else does.

**Sizing:** A typical residential French drain in Las Cruces uses a 4-inch perforated pipe in a 12–18 inch wide trench, 18–24 inches deep (or deeper if needed to reach below a shallow caliche layer). The trench is lined with landscape fabric to keep soil from clogging the gravel, filled with ¾-inch drain rock, and topped with soil or gravel to match the surrounding grade.

## Solution 3: Catch Basins and Channel Drains

Where surface water concentrates — at the bottom of a slope, where a driveway meets a patio, along a retaining wall — catch basins and channel drains collect it and pipe it away.

**Catch basins** are boxes set flush with the ground surface, covered with a grate. Water flows in from the surface, collects in the box, and drains through a connected pipe to a discharge point. They work well at specific collection points — the low corner of a patio, the base of a downspout run, or the bottom of a sloped yard.

**Channel drains** (also called trench drains) are narrow, linear drains set into hardscape surfaces. They're ideal along the edge of a driveway, across a walkway, or at the threshold of a garage — anywhere a line of water crosses a paved surface. The channel collects the water across its full length and drains it to a connected pipe.

Both connect to the same discharge infrastructure as a French drain — a sloped pipe running to a lower-elevation outlet. In Las Cruces, make sure the pipe run actually reaches a discharge point and doesn't dead-end underground above caliche.

## Solution 4: Dry Creek Beds and Swales

For properties where monsoon runoff flows across the yard — particularly on sloped lots in the Organ Mountain foothills, Picacho Hills, or the east mesa — a dry creek bed or swale is often the most practical and attractive solution.

**Dry creek beds** are shallow, rock-lined channels designed to direct surface water. During a storm, they function as drainage channels. The rest of the year, they look like a natural desert feature — river rock winding through the landscape. They work with the natural grade of the property, collecting water from higher ground and routing it to a discharge point or infiltration area.

Design considerations for Las Cruces:

**Rock sizing.** Use rock large enough to stay in place during heavy flow. For most residential applications, 4–8 inch river rock in the channel bed with larger boulders along the edges works well. Pea gravel and small decorative rock will wash downstream during the first serious monsoon.

**Width and depth.** Size the channel for the volume of water you expect. A general rule: the channel should be at least twice as wide as the anticipated flow width, with enough depth that water doesn't spill over the sides during a heavy storm. For a typical residential lot, 18–24 inches wide and 6–12 inches deep handles most monsoon events.

**Discharge.** The creek bed needs to end somewhere — ideally the street, an arroyo, or a designated low area where water can infiltrate. A dry creek bed that dead-ends in a flat part of the yard just moves the problem downstream.

**Swales** are shallower, wider, vegetated versions of the same concept. A swale planted with deep-rooted native grasses or groundcovers slows runoff, allows more infiltration, and can be integrated into the landscape design as a planted feature rather than a rock channel. Swales work best on gentler slopes where you want to slow water down rather than move it quickly.

## When Multiple Solutions Work Together

Most Las Cruces properties with drainage problems need a combination of approaches, not a single fix:

**Regrading + French drain:** Correct the surface grade to move water away from the house, then install a French drain where subsurface water collects. This handles both surface and underground water.

**Dry creek bed + catch basin:** A dry creek bed channels monsoon runoff across the yard to a catch basin near the property line, which connects to a pipe discharging to the street or an arroyo.

**Regrading + dry creek bed + planting:** Re-slope the problem area, install a dry creek bed to handle concentrated flow, and plant [deep-rooted desert species](/blog/desert-plants-las-cruces/) along the margins to stabilize the soil and increase infiltration over time.

The key is understanding where water is coming from, where it's going (or where it should go), and what's blocking it. A walkthrough of your property during or immediately after a monsoon storm tells you more than any amount of dry-weather planning.

## What to Do After Monsoon Season Shows You the Problem

If this monsoon season revealed drainage problems you didn't know you had — water against the foundation, a yard that stayed soggy for days, gravel washed into places it doesn't belong, erosion along a slope — October is the time to fix it.

The ground is still workable from monsoon moisture. The weather is comfortable for outdoor work. And you want drainage solutions in place before [fall planting](/blog/fall-prep-las-cruces/) — there's no point putting new trees and shrubs into a yard that floods.

Start by walking the property and noting where water collects, where it flows, and where it exits (or fails to exit). Check the grade near the foundation with a level. Look for erosion patterns that show where concentrated flow is running. Dig a test hole in the problem area and see what's underneath — if you hit caliche, that's your answer for why it's not draining.
