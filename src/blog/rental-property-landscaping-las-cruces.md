If you own rental property in Las Cruces — whether it's a single-family home in Elks Club, a duplex near Telshor, or a handful of units on the east mesa — you've probably figured out that landscaping is one of those things that either runs smoothly or becomes a constant headache. There's not much middle ground.

The good news is that Las Cruces is one of the easier places in the country to get rental property landscaping right, because the desert climate actually works in your favor — if you set things up correctly from the start. The bad news is that most rental properties aren't set up correctly, and landlords end up paying for it through water bills, code violations, or turnover.

## What Code Enforcement Actually Looks For

Las Cruces code enforcement isn't going to fine you for a yard that doesn't look like a magazine cover. They care about one thing: nuisances. Under the city's municipal code (Chapter 18), "noxious weeds and other rank vegetation" are explicitly classified as a nuisance. If your rental property has weeds that are interfering with neighboring properties, creating hiding spots for rodents and snakes, or just visibly overtaking the lot, you're looking at a citation.

The penalty: up to $500 per violation, and each day the violation continues counts as a separate offense. A week of ignoring a weed notice can turn into $3,500.

Code enforcement responds to complaints — they're not driving through neighborhoods looking for tall weeds. But it only takes one neighbor calling to start the clock. And rental properties get reported more than owner-occupied homes because tenants often don't think weed control is their problem (and without a clear lease clause, they may be right).

The monsoon season makes this worse. July through September, Las Cruces gets enough rain to turn bare dirt into a weed field in two weeks. If nobody's managing your rental property's yard during monsoon season, you're likely getting a call from either your tenant or code enforcement.

## Design for the Desert, Not Against It

The single biggest mistake landlords make with rental property landscaping in Las Cruces is trying to maintain a grass lawn. Grass needs constant water, regular mowing, fertilizing, aeration, and a tenant who actually does all of that. Most tenants don't. And when they move out, you inherit a dead lawn that's expensive to replace and impossible to justify for the next lease cycle.

The better approach: [xeriscape](/services/landscaping/xeriscaping/).

A properly installed xeriscape — [desert-adapted plants](/blog/desert-plants-las-cruces/), [decomposed granite or gravel](/services/landscaping/gravel-rock/) groundcover, [drip irrigation](/services/irrigation/drip-irrigation/), and landscape fabric underneath — looks maintained year-round with almost no effort. No mowing. No fertilizing. Minimal watering. The upfront cost is comparable to re-sodding, but the ongoing cost drops dramatically.

For rental properties specifically, xeriscape solves the tenant problem. You're no longer depending on a renter to mow the lawn every week or adjust the sprinkler timer for seasonal changes. A drip system on a timer waters the plants. Pre-emergent herbicide in February and September keeps weeds under control. That's it.

If you do want some green — maybe the backyard is fenced and tenants want usable space — consider [artificial turf](/services/landscaping/artificial-turf/) in high-visibility areas and xeriscape everywhere else. Turf looks green year-round, drains well, and requires zero watering. For a rental property, that combination means the yard always looks occupied and maintained, whether the tenant cares about landscaping or not.

## Irrigation: The Silent Money Pit

Irrigation is where rental properties lose the most money without landlords realizing it. Here's what typically happens: a sprinkler head gets hit by a car tire or cracked by the sun, it starts spraying water onto the sidewalk or running continuously, nobody reports it, and the next water bill is triple what it should be. In Las Cruces, where water isn't cheap, this adds up fast.

The problems multiply with the city's [watering schedule restrictions](/blog/summer-survival-guide-las-cruces-landscape/#the-watering-schedule-you-have-to-follow). Between April and September, outdoor watering is only allowed before 10 a.m. and after 6 p.m., on your designated days (even addresses: Tuesday, Thursday, Saturday; odd addresses: Wednesday, Friday, Sunday). A tenant who doesn't know the schedule — or a timer that's set wrong — can trigger a city violation on top of the wasted water.

The fix is straightforward:

**Switch to drip irrigation.** Drip systems deliver water directly to plant roots through emitters, not through spray heads that water the sidewalk, the fence, and the neighbor's driveway. They use a fraction of the water, they're harder for tenants to accidentally damage, and they work perfectly with xeriscape. Common [drip irrigation problems](/blog/irrigation-problems-las-cruces/) — clogged emitters from hard water, cracked tubing from UV exposure — are easy to catch and cheap to fix during a seasonal walkthrough.

**Install a smart controller.** A [smart irrigation controller](/blog/smart-irrigation-controllers-las-cruces/) adjusts watering based on weather data. It won't water the day after a monsoon storm. It automatically follows the city's watering schedule. And most models let you check and adjust the schedule from your phone — meaning you can verify your rental property's irrigation is running correctly without driving over there.

**Seasonal tune-ups.** An [irrigation tune-up](/services/irrigation/seasonal-tune-ups/) in spring and fall catches broken heads, clogged emitters, leaks, and timer malfunctions. For a rental property, this is preventive maintenance that pays for itself in avoided water waste and code compliance.

## The Lease Is Your Best Tool

The most common landlord landscaping problem isn't a design issue or an irrigation issue — it's a lease issue. If your lease doesn't clearly assign landscaping responsibilities, your tenant assumes you're handling it, and you assume they are, and nobody's handling it until code enforcement shows up.

**What to specify in the lease:**

Who mows (if there's grass), how often, and what happens if it doesn't get done. Who pulls weeds. Who's responsible for reporting irrigation issues. Whether the tenant can plant anything or modify the landscaping. Who handles tree trimming. What constitutes "maintained in good condition."

For xeriscape properties, the lease clause is simpler because there's less to maintain. A reasonable clause might read: "Tenant is responsible for removing visible weeds and reporting any irrigation malfunctions within 48 hours. Landlord is responsible for seasonal irrigation maintenance and pre-emergent weed treatment."

This kind of split works because it puts the daily observation responsibility on the person who's actually there (the tenant), while keeping the skilled maintenance tasks with you or your contractor. The tenant doesn't need to know how to fix a drip emitter — they just need to tell you when the plants start dying.

## What to Prioritize by Property Type

Not every rental property needs the same approach. What makes sense depends on the property, the neighborhood, and the tenants you're targeting.

**Single-family homes in established neighborhoods** (University Hills, Alameda, Elks Club): These neighborhoods have expectations. A completely bare gravel yard with no plants will stand out and may affect your ability to attract quality tenants. A clean xeriscape with foundation plantings, a few accent plants, and maintained gravel looks like a homeowner lives there. That's the goal.

**Properties near NMSU or in student-heavy areas**: Durability over aesthetics. Students aren't maintaining a landscape. Go with gravel or decomposed granite, a few bulletproof plants (Red Yucca, Desert Spoon, Agave), and drip irrigation on a timer. Design it so it looks fine with zero tenant effort.

**Newer subdivisions with HOAs** (Sonoma Ranch, Metro Verde, Picacho Hills): HOA guidelines may dictate minimum plant coverage, approved materials, and maintenance standards. Review the CC&Rs before you do anything — our [HOA xeriscaping guide](/blog/hoa-friendly-xeriscaping-las-cruces/) covers what you need to know about ARC approval and design requirements that pass review.

**Multi-unit properties and small complexes**: Common areas are your responsibility regardless of what the lease says. A [landscape maintenance contract](/services/landscape-maintenance/) for common areas ensures consistent upkeep and keeps you ahead of any code issues. Individual unit yards can be tenant-maintained with clear lease language.

## Turnover Is Where It Falls Apart

The gap between tenants is when rental property landscaping goes sideways. A two-week vacancy in July means two weeks of no watering and explosive weed growth. By the time you're showing the property to the next tenant, the yard looks abandoned.

**Before listing a vacant property:**

Pull or spray all visible weeds. Run the irrigation system and check for leaks or broken emitters. Rake the gravel back into place if monsoon runoff has pushed it around. Trim anything overgrown. Remove any dead plants.

This is a half-day of work for one person — or a single visit from a landscaping crew. The difference in first impressions is worth significantly more than the cost of the cleanup.

If you're doing a full landscape renovation between tenants, that's the time to convert from grass to xeriscape. The property is empty, the crew has access, and you won't be fighting the watering schedule while tenants are living there. [Planning that project in advance](/blog/planning-landscape-project-las-cruces/) means it can happen during the turnover window instead of becoming a separate project you keep pushing off.

## When a Maintenance Contract Makes Sense

If you own one rental property and it's nearby, you can probably handle the landscaping yourself with a few hours per season. But the math changes quickly:

**Multiple properties.** If you're managing three or more rentals, the drive time and coordination of seasonal maintenance across all of them starts to justify a quarterly maintenance contract.

**Out-of-town ownership.** If you don't live in Las Cruces — or you live here but travel regularly — you need someone checking the properties. Irrigation leaks, weed growth, and code violations don't wait for you to get back in town.

**Properties with HOA requirements.** HOA violations come with their own fines and timelines. A maintenance contractor who knows your HOA's standards keeps you compliant without you having to track the requirements yourself.

**Tenant turnover.** A standing relationship with a landscaper means you can schedule a turnover cleanup as soon as you know the move-out date, rather than scrambling to find someone available during your listing window.

A quarterly maintenance visit — checking irrigation, applying pre-emergent, pulling weeds, trimming plants, checking for drainage issues — catches problems when they're small. A broken emitter is a quick fix in March. The same broken emitter, ignored until August, is a dead plant and a bare spot you have to explain to the HOA and the next tenant.

It handles the heat, [survives the wind](/blog/monsoon-prep-las-cruces/), stays compliant with watering restrictions, and looks maintained with minimal effort.
